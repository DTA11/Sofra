Jars for preserves: washing, sterilising and the water bath

Preserves spoil because of microbes left in the jar or let in by a bad lid. Clean hot jars, hot food and boiling the closed jars in a pot of water deal with almost all of it — and it does not take long.

In short

  1. Jars without cracks or chipped rims; lids new, or free of rust, dents and damaged seals.
  2. Wash them in hot soapy water, then sterilise: 20 minutes in the oven at 120 °C or 10 minutes in boiling water. Boil the lids for 5 minutes.
  3. Hot food into hot jars: up to 1 cm below the rim (tomato passata and compote 1.5 cm).
  4. Wipe the rim and close, then boil the closed jars in a pot of water — times are in the table below.
  5. Next day, check: the lid is pulled in and does not click when pressed.

How long to boil the closed jars

Time it from the moment the water comes back to the boil. The water must be 2–3 cm above the lids.

If you live above 300 m, add 5 minutes; above 900 m add 10 minutes — water boils at a lower temperature up there.

Step by step

1. Check jars and lids

Run a finger round the rim of every jar — a chip or crack means it will not seal or will break in the pot. Throw away lids with rust, dents or a cracked seal. A lid can be reused only if it is perfectly flat and clean; a new one is safest.

2. Wash

Wash jars and lids in hot soapy water, rinse in hot water and stand upside down on a clean cloth. Take off old labels — dirt collects under them.

3. Sterilise

In the oven: put the wet jars on a rack in a cold oven, heat to 120 °C and hold for 20 minutes. Leave them inside until you fill them. In water: submerge the jars in a pot of water, bring to the boil and boil for 10 minutes. Always boil the lids in water (5 minutes) — the oven damages the seal.

4. Fill

Pour the hot preserve into hot jars — a cold jar can crack with boiling food. Leave the stated space to the rim, press out air bubbles with a spoon or chopstick, wipe the rim with a clean damp cloth and close at once. Screw the lid on firmly by hand, not with force.

5. Water bath

Put a cloth or rack on the bottom of a large pot (the jars must not stand directly on the base). Stand the jars so they do not touch, pour in hot water to 2–3 cm above the lids, cover the pot and bring to the boil. Start timing when it boils. When the time is up, turn off the heat and leave the jars in the water for 5 more minutes.

6. Cool and check

Lift the jars out upright with tongs or a cloth and stand them on a wooden board or cloth, away from draughts. Do not press the lids while they cool — you will hear a click as they pull in. Next day check every jar: the lid is dipped and does not move when pressed. Write what it is and the date on the jar.

What about turning jars upside down and wrapping them in blankets?

Plenty of people do it, and for jam, plum butter and pickles it usually works. But it is not the same as a water bath: the contents do not heat evenly to the middle of the jar. For ajvar, tomato passata and anything made of low-acid vegetables, boiling the closed jars is a must.

When something goes wrong

A jar broke in the pot
It was cold going into hot water, or it stood directly on the base. Next time keep the jars and water at a similar temperature, with a cloth or rack underneath.
The lid did not pull in
The jar has not sealed. Within 24 hours you can process it again with a new lid, or put it in the fridge and use it within 2 weeks.
The contents leaked during boiling
The jar was too full or the lid too loose. Leave the stated space. If the lid pulled in afterwards anyway, wipe the jar and store it as normal.
The top layer has darkened
Too much air was left in the jar. If the lid is pulled in and it smells normal, it is not dangerous — next time press out the bubbles and fill to the right level.

Storage

Keep closed jars in a dark, cool, dry place (5–15 °C). An opened jar goes into the fridge. More: How to store preserves and how long they keep.
